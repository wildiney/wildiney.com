One third of people who have ever talked to an AI chatbot told it a secret they never told anyone else. Not their doctor. Not their family.

The data comes from a study commissioned by DuckDuckGo with 1,944 adults in the United States. Parents and guardians with a child at home share secrets with chatbots at almost twice the rate: 43%, compared to 25% among those with no child at home. That makes sense: raising a child raises questions that are hard to confess, and so far chatbots do not give "that" judgmental look.

But 53% of respondents did not even know that training the model on their own conversations is industry standard, and only 25% knew that this history can become evidence in legal proceedings. They trusted because no one had told them otherwise. When they found out: 58% reported discomfort and 30% reported high discomfort.

Training is left on by default rather than requiring explicit consent, opt-out is buried in settings, free accounts carry looser retention policies than paid ones, and all of this is a decision made by whoever designed the product.

Whoever designs the product has an outcome in mind, whether facilitating, making difficult, or biasing the user's choice.
